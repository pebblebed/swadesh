Swadesh working notes · II · on method

One row per language

A neural-first route to relatedness — learn a vector per language by reconstruction, then read kinship off the embedding space. Who has done it, and what it actually measures.

Companion to Note I, Coherence, not closeness

The proposal: give every language one learned row in an embedding table; pass that row through a shared decoder trained to emit the language's Swadesh list; then measure relatedness as distance between the rows. It has a precise name in the literature — learning a per-language embedding from a generation objective and reading kinship off the embedding space — and there is a bullseye precedent plus a tight cluster around it.

Why it works at allA shared decoder + a low-dimensional code is an information bottleneck. Languages the decoder can express as small perturbations of one another are pushed close together — the same job a factor model's low rank performs.

The bullseyeThis has been done, and it recovered trees

Östling & Tiedemann (2017), "Continuous multilinguality with language vectors." One shared character-level LSTM language model, conditioned on a learned per-language embedding, trained over ~975 languages of Bible translations. The only per-language degree of freedom is that vector — exactly your design, with a free-text decoder in place of a Swadesh decoder. The payoff is your hypothesis confirmed: the learned vectors cluster genealogically and reconstruct a plausible phylogenetic tree from cosine distance. Strong evidence the idea works in principle.

The surrounding cluster

Malaviya ’17
Learning Language Representations for Typology Prediction. Language vectors learned from an NMT system, then probed for typology. Same "language as a learned conditioning vector."
Murawaki ’15
Continuous-Space Representations of Linguistic Typology. Latent per-language vectors → phylogenetic inference. The Bayesian-latent analog of your idea.
Littell ’17
URIEL / lang2vec. The instructive contrast: language vectors curated from typological databases rather than learned from raw data. Same banner, opposite provenance.
B-Côté ’13
Automated reconstruction of ancient languages (PNAS). Generative phylogenetics: latent tree + proto-forms generate the word lists. Your decoder is the tree-free, Euclidean relaxation.
x-vectors ’18
Speaker embeddings (i-/x-vectors; TTS speaker & style codes). The cross-domain twin: code → decoder → output, similarity read in code space. Languages are your speakers.
Bjerva ’19
What do language representations really represent? The cautionary analysis: learned language vectors entangle genealogy, areal contact, and typology.

Abstractly, the design is factor analysis — or a VAE — over a (language × concept) matrix: make the code a distribution and you have a VAE over languages; keep it a lookup table and it is MAP factor analysis. "lang2vec, learned from reconstruction loss."

The catchIt likely measures the wrong thing

…and the "wrong thing" is the exact axis Note I was about.

1

Surface, not correspondence.

A free decoder conditioned on a code has no inductive bias to discover that k : t is a cheap, systematic transformation; it will just as happily memorize Rapa Nui's strings and Tahitian's strings separately. So embedding distance tracks average surface resemblance plus lexical replacement — conflating inheritance, borrowing, and chance, the very things the comparative method separates. This is neural lexicostatistics — closer to ASJP than to the rule-coverage idea you liked.

2

Thin data, entangled signal.

Östling–Tiedemann had ~1000 languages × whole Bibles. You have ~1200 lists × ~200 words, concept-aligned. Two hundred tokens per language is sparse enough that a free embedding may just encode "which exact strings appear." And learned language vectors notoriously mix genealogy with areal contact and typology, so raw distance is not clean relatedness.

The lever you haveConcept alignment. Östling–Tiedemann had none; you do. It lets a shared per-concept structure carry most of the load, leaving the language vector to encode only deviations.

The fixMake the bottleneck phonological

Two moves turn this from neural lexicostatistics into something correspondence-aware. First, exploit the concept alignment with a two-way factor model — form ≈ g(z_language, c_concept) — so the per-concept factor carries the lexical load and z_language only needs to encode systematic deviation. That regularizes the thin data and cleans the distance.

Second, and decisively: make the per-language code parameterize a structured transformation — a feature-rule or transducer layer — rather than a free decoder. Then the bottleneck is forced to encode "apply these sound correspondences," not memorized forms. That is the amortized, neural form of Note I's "optimal simple ruleset," and the learnable cousin of Bouchard-Côté's parameterized sound-change process.

The two designs are the ends of one axis — interpretable-and-correspondence-aware versus distributed-and-surface. The interesting object is the midpoint: a neural language embedding whose decoder is constrained to be a phonological-correspondence engine.

VerdictRight family, watch the objective

In short

Yes — clear analogs exist, and the canonical one (Östling & Tiedemann) validates that a per-language embedding learned through a shared decoder recovers genealogy. The architecture is sound.

But a free decoder will give you a smarter ASJP, not a comparative method — surface distance, on thin data, with genealogy and contact entangled. The value is unlocked by constraining the bottleneck: concept-factored, and structured so the code expresses correspondences rather than forms.

Run it on the four Polynesian sisters we actually hold — rap · tah · smo · ton, all already IPA — and the test is sharp: does code distance recover k : ʔ and *f, *s → h, or does it just rank lists by how similar their letters happen to look?
